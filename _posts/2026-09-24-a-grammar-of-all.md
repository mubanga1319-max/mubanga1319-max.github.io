In English, "all" is a neutral word. It attaches to anything without changing: all the people, all the things, all the time. In Bemba, there is no neutral "all". The word, built on the stem *-onse*, changes its shape to match what is being gathered.

| Bemba | Meaning | What is gathered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *bonse* | everyone | people |
| *fyonse* | everything | things |
| *tonse* | all the small things | small things |
| *lyonse* | always | time |
| *ponse / konse / monse* | at every point / everywhere / throughout | place |

Each time a Bemba speaker says "all", the grammar asks a question first: all of what kind? People, things, small things, time, or one of three kinds of space?

## Whole and all

There is a second lesson hidden here. With a single thing, the same stem means "whole": *umuti onse* is the whole tree. With many things, it means "all": *imiti yonse* is all the trees. Bemba treats wholeness and allness as two faces of one idea.

## An old inheritance

This is not a Bemba curiosity. The same stem runs through the Bantu family: Nyanja *onse* and *zonse*, Swahili *wote* and *vyote*, Zulu *bonke*. Swahili even has *pote*, *kote* and *mote*, matching Bemba's three words for place. A classification of reality has been carried in everyday speech across a continent for thousands of years.

## Why this matters

Colonial linguistics often described African languages as simple or concrete, lacking abstraction. A grammar that systematically distinguishes persons, things, scale, time and three kinds of space is the opposite of simple. It is knowledge, organised and transmitted without a single textbook.

> Bantu languages deserve to be studied as knowledge systems, with the same care we give to any other science.

I am preparing a research paper on this paradigm and a wider research programme: how noun classes classify reality, how locative words shape spatial reasoning, and how our languages could strengthen science teaching in mother tongues. If you speak a Bantu language, you already hold part of the data.
