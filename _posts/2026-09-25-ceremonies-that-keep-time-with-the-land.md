Zambia's traditional ceremonies are usually presented as tourism events or moments of cultural pride. Both are true. But read closely, four of them are also something else: living archives of how our peoples learned to live with water, harvest and land.

## Kuomboka: governing with the flood

Kuomboka means "to get out of the water". At the end of the rains, as the Zambezi floods the Barotse plain, the Litunga of the Lozi moves from Lealui on the floodplain to Limulunga on higher ground. The date is not fixed by calendar. It follows the water. Authority does not fight the flood; it moves with it.

## Ncwala: gratitude before consumption

Ncwala, held by the Ngoni near Chipata in February, is a first-fruits ceremony. Traditionally, no one ate the new crops until the Paramount Chief had received and blessed them. After the hungry months, a whole community practised restraint and gratitude before appetite. The colonial administration banned Ncwala after the Ngoni resisted British rule; it was revived in 1980.

## Lwiindi: leadership bound to rain

Lwiindi, the main ceremony of the Tonga, is held at the Gonde shrine in Monze each July. It gives thanks for the rain and harvest of the past season and asks for good rains in the next. Tradition holds that the first Chief Monze was a rainmaker. A chief's legitimacy rests on his relationship with the rain, not on accumulation.

## Mutomboko: a kingdom rooted in a tree

Mutomboko, the victory dance of the Lunda of Mwata Kazembe at Mwansabombwe, is first a ceremony of history and sovereignty. It remembers the migration and the crossing of the Luapula. Yet ecology sits at its foundation: a cutting of the Mutaba tree was planted at the new capital, and the kingdom was sustained by the fisheries of Lake Mweru.

## What they share

- Authority tied to natural cycles: flood, harvest, rain and land.
- Timing set by nature rather than by the calendar.
- Gratitude before consumption.
- Memory made public, taught to every generation.

> Africa does not need saving. It needs remembering.

Today, the nations that did most to disrupt these systems return to advise Africa on sustainability. There is value in modern science and technology. But ecological wisdom was never absent here. It was written in our ceremonies, our totems and our farming long before the phrase "carbon footprint" existed.
