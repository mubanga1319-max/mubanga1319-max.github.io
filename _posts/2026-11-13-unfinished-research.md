I recently read *Imilimo ya Bena Kale*, and it has not let me go. It challenged me, and then it compelled me to contribute to what our ancestors discovered, and to study the environment right where we are.

One question keeps returning: **how did they know?**

How did they know *amankolobwe* were food? How did they know *amasuku*, *ifishimu*, *imfungo* and *chikanda* could be eaten? These are indigenous foods you will not find in the science books. Some of them do not even have an English name.

Think of *chikanda* alone. Someone had to find a small tuber hidden beneath the ground and recognise it as food. Then they had to discover that, pounded and cooked with groundnuts, it sets into a meal that has fed generations. That is not luck. That is experiment, observation and recipe: knowledge built step by step.

Somebody found out. Someone tasted what had never been tasted. Someone watched which caterpillars were safe and in which season. Someone learned which roots heal and which kill. Some of them died so that we could know what to eat and what to leave alone. That knowledge was paid for with lives. That effort cannot be in vain.

The same is true of the stories our grandmothers told by the fire. Folklore does not make itself. Every tale was crafted, told, corrected and passed down because it carried something worth keeping: a warning, a law, a way of living on this land.

So what was it all for? Because we have now travelled the world and learned to speak English, do we conclude that they wasted their time?

## Not superstition, but unfinished research

All the myths and superstitions we have expelled from our lives are, I believe, **unfinished science-based research projects**. They are observations about soil, rain, animals, plants and people, recorded in the only archive our ancestors had: language, ritual and story. They were not wrong. They were interrupted.

That interruption was no accident. Colonial education carried a hidden curriculum. To be considered educated, an African had to speak like the European, dress like him and think like him. Our own knowledge was renamed "superstition" and left outside the classroom door. That curriculum outlived the colonial office, and it still shapes what we call knowledge today.

## Who will defend them?

We send our brightest to European and American universities to master European philosophies and traditions, and they return with PhDs to defend them. If we can defend the philosophies of others with such dedication, then I must ask:

**Who will defend my grandmother's theories? Who will defend my grandfather's philosophy?**

I would rather stay and defend my ancestors' work than rush to Harvard and be whitewashed.

## Back to the record

For a year now I have been reading at the National Archives, going through records of how our people lived, farmed, governed and explained the world. Every file confirms what I already suspected: there is a body of knowledge here waiting to be taken seriously, as research and not as curiosity.

I owe much of this courage to the late Dr Chishimba Lumbwe, my favourite writer of all time. He showed me that we can question the categories we inherited and think for ourselves about who we are.

## An alternative school of thought

We need an alternative school of thought. It is not a rejection of science. It is science that starts here: from our foods and forests, from our languages, which carry their own logic (as I argued in [A grammar of "all"]({{ '/journal/a-grammar-of-all/' | relative_url }})), from the history our clans carry (see [Rain, clay and trees]({{ '/journal/rain-clay-and-trees/' | relative_url }})), and from our elders' explanations. In this school, a grandmother's warning is a hypothesis to be tested, not a superstition to be laughed at.

That is what Ilyashi pa Nsaka is for. If your elders taught you something about a wild food, a plant, a season or a taboo, write it down and [share it with me]({{ '/research/' | relative_url }}). Ask your grandparents this week, before that knowledge leaves with them.

The future is not in mimicry. It is in memory. The research is unfinished. Let us finish it.
