Before colonial rule, the peoples who now make up Zambia did not organise themselves into provinces called Northern, Southern or Luapula. Those are administrative boundaries, drawn to make taxation and indirect rule easier. Over time, the administrative map became a social map, and in many elections it became a political map too.

Communities once connected by trade, migration and intermarriage were catalogued into separate "tribes". The writer Dr Chishimba Lumbwe has pointed out that England treats its own regional accents, Cockney, Yorkshire, Geordie and Scouse, as variations within one nation, while in Central Africa closely related speech communities were often classified as separate peoples.

## One identity the map never contained

There is one institution colonial administration never fully reorganised: the clan. Clans do not respect provincial borders. Members of the Mumba and Mfula clans, for example, are found among several peoples who speak different languages. Two strangers from opposite ends of the country can discover that they are kin.

Political scientists call these cross-cutting identities. Societies are more stable when people who are divided in one way are connected in another. Zambia already has such a connection. It simply has never been given official recognition.

## A modest proposal

Zambia is moving from the paper National Registration Card to a digital card under the Integrated National Registration Information System. Every citizen is being re-enrolled. That creates a rare chance to add one field, at almost no cost:

> An optional, self-declared Clan / Totem field, which can be left blank and is never used to grant or deny anything.

The card already records a holder's village and chief, fields that place us within administrative units. A clan field would record something that connects us across them.

## Safeguards first

Any identity field can be misused, so the design matters as much as the idea. The field should be voluntary. It should be protected under data protection law and never used for employment, services, land or elections. Citizens who do not know their clan, or who come from families with different traditions, should always be free to leave it blank.

A national clan register, compiled with the House of Chiefs, historians and linguists, would list each clan's name in the main Zambian languages. It would show citizens where their clan is shared.

## Remembering, not inventing

This proposal does not abolish provinces, languages or ethnic identities, and it does not diminish traditional leadership. It asks the state to recognise something Zambians have carried all along. Perhaps the path to a stronger One Zambia, One Nation begins there.
